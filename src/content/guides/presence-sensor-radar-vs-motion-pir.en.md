A light that switches off while you are sitting at the desk is the classic motion sensor complaint. A presence sensor based on radar is the usual answer. It is not better in every room, though, and it has its own quirks. Here is how to choose.

## Two kinds of sensor

- **A motion sensor (PIR)** detects the change in heat from a warm body moving across its view. It is simple, cheap to run and runs on a battery for a long time. It reports movement, and goes quiet when you keep still.
- **A radar presence sensor** sends out low-power radio waves, usually in the 60 GHz millimetre-wave band, and reads how they bounce back. It can notice small movements such as breathing, so it can report that someone is still in the room when they are sitting or lying down. It usually needs mains power.

Neither records a picture or sound.

## Which room suits which

- **Corridors, entrances and stairs:** a motion sensor. People are moving, you want a quick response, and a battery unit is easy to fit.
- **Bathrooms and toilets:** often radar, because someone can be in the shower or sitting still for a while. A radar sensor near a shower needs care: steam and moisture are a question for the sensor's maker, so check that it is rated for the place.
- **Living room and study:** radar, because people sit still for long stretches.
- **Bedroom:** radar can help with night lighting and with an aircon that should stay on while someone sleeps. Place it so it does not look at a ceiling fan or at the next room.
- **Store rooms and utility areas:** a motion sensor is usually enough.

## What can go wrong

- **Radar sees through some walls and thin partitions,** so a sensor facing a thin wall may report someone in the next room. Aim it into the room, and use the sensor's zone or range setting to cut off the area behind the wall.
- **Moving objects confuse it.** A ceiling fan, a curtain in the breeze or a pet can trigger either type. Pointing it away and setting a sensitivity level usually fixes this.
- **A motion sensor loses someone who is still.** Lengthen the hold time, or add a second signal, such as the door sensor.
- **Mounting position matters a lot.** Height and angle change the coverage for both types. Test before you fix anything permanently.

## Settings that matter more than the model

1. **Hold time.** How long the room stays "occupied" after the last reading. Too short and the light goes off on you. Too long and the aircon runs for an empty room.
2. **Zone or range.** The area the sensor counts. Trim it to the room.
3. **Sensitivity.** Lower it if a fan or a pet keeps triggering it.
4. **Separate delays for different jobs.** A light can react quickly, while an aircon should wait longer before switching off.

## Common questions

### Is a radar presence sensor safe to have in a bedroom?

It uses low-power radio waves and records no picture or sound. Choose a product from a reputable maker that states its compliance with the relevant regulations in Singapore, and ask the supplier for it.

### Can I use radar to count people?

Some sensors can estimate several people in a zone, but accuracy varies. For counting that matters, such as in a shop, ask for how it was measured in a space like yours.

### Do radar sensors work on batteries?

Most need mains power or a USB supply, because they measure all the time. Motion sensors are the usual battery choice.

### Will a pet trigger it?

It can. Sensitivity and zone settings, and the mounting position, usually reduce false triggers. Test with the pet in the room.

## A simple way to start

Replace one problem sensor first: the bathroom, the study or the corridor light that switches off at the wrong time. Adjust the hold time and zone over a week or two, then decide whether to do the rest.
