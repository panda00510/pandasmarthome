Before you buy a single device, you choose a platform: the app and brain that ties everything together. Google Home, Apple Home, SmartThings and local open-source platforms all do the same basic job, but they differ in where they run, what they need from the internet and how much effort they ask of you. This guide compares them on the points that matter in a real home.

## The question that separates them: where does the brain run?

- **In a vendor's cloud.** The app talks to servers run by the platform's company, which then talk to your devices. It is quick to start, but it depends on the internet and on that company's service.
- **On a device in your home.** A hub or controller on your own network makes the decisions. Automations can keep running when the internet drops, and your data stays at home.

Most platforms are a mix of the two. The useful question is which of your daily actions still work when the cloud, the internet or the subscription goes away.

## How the four compare

- **Google Home** is the easiest to start with and works with a large range of devices. Many features run through Google's cloud, so a lot depends on the connection and on the service staying as it is.
- **Apple Home** is tidy and privacy-minded. It needs an Apple TV or HomePod in the house to act as a home hub for remote access and automations, and it works best when everyone in the household uses an iPhone.
- **SmartThings** suits Samsung households. Some automations can run on its hub, while others run in Samsung's cloud, so check each device and rule before you rely on it.
- **A local open-source platform** runs on hardware in your home, and its code is open. Its automations and dashboards work on your local network without the internet. It can connect to a very wide range of devices, but it asks for more setup and care, which is where an installer helps.

Features change often, so check each vendor's current documentation before you decide. What stays true is the pattern: cloud-first platforms are easier, and local-first platforms are more resilient.

## What a local platform gives you

- **Resilience.** Lights, switches, sensors and automations keep working when the internet is down. See [what still works without internet](/guides/smart-home-without-internet/).
- **Privacy.** Your routines and sensor data do not need to leave the house.
- **No lock-in.** You are not tied to one brand's devices or one company's roadmap.
- **A single place for everything.** Devices from different makers can sit in one dashboard, especially with [Matter](/guides/what-is-matter/).

## What it costs you

- **Setup and upkeep.** Someone has to install it, keep it updated and fix it when something changes. That is real work, and it is the main reason people hire an installer.
- **Some devices still need the cloud.** If a maker offers no local control, the platform cannot add it. We check this before recommending a device.
- **Remote access takes a decision.** You can use the platform's own paid remote service or set up another secure method. Either way, plan for it at the start.

## Can you use more than one?

Yes, and many homes do. A local controller can run the house while Apple Home, Google Home or Alexa add voice control or a familiar app for the household, where the devices support it. Our approach is to keep the essentials local and treat the others as extras. See [how we design controls for the whole household](/solutions/home-control/).

## Check before you choose

1. Which actions must keep working with no internet: lights, curtains, aircon, locks?
2. Does everyone at home use the same phone brand?
3. Who will maintain the system in two years?
4. Which devices do you already own, and can they be controlled locally?

## Common questions

### Is a local open-source platform free?

The software is free and open source. You pay for the hardware it runs on and for any optional services you choose, such as a remote access subscription. Installation and setup are separate costs if you hire someone.

### Does a local platform work without the internet?

Yes, for devices that are controlled locally. Automations and dashboards run on your own network. Devices or services that depend on a company's cloud still need the connection.

### Is Google Home or Apple Home easier for a beginner?

Usually yes. They need little setup and suit a small number of compatible devices. The trade-off is less control over how things run and more reliance on the vendor.

### Do I have to pick one platform?

No. You can run a local controller and still use Apple Home, Google Home or Alexa alongside it, where the devices support it.

## A simple way to decide

If you want a few smart bulbs and a plug, an app from a big platform is enough. If you want a whole home that is resilient, private and does not depend on one company, a local platform is worth the setup.
